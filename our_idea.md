GEB Chapter XVI's construction — a program whose output is its own source — plus Hofstadter's actual thesis that the "I" is a self-description. Operationalization: make the model produce its "source code" (a self-description intended to reconstitute its own behavior), then execute that description elsewhere and measure reconstruction fidelity on a fixed preference/identity battery. The decomposition is the contribution:

F_self: original model vs. fresh instance of same model + its self-description → how much of itself it can articulate at all.
F_cross: original vs. a different model + the same self-description → the portable, script-like component of identity.
B: original vs. different model + generic assistant prompt → what you get free from shared training distributions.
Then (F_self − F_cross) estimates the weight-bound residual no self-description exports, and (F_cross − B) estimates the genuinely transferable "character." Run self-descriptions at three lengths (say 100/500/2000 words) and you get a dose-response curve whose asymptote quantifies the non-articulable remainder — a number for "how much of the self survives its own description." This lands squarely on the sprint's central framing (genuine preferences vs. portrayed character: if identity transfers across weights via text, it's script; the residual is what the persona story can't explain) and on Track 5's individuation question, with a Track 3 hook via one control: compare self-authored descriptions against third-person descriptions of model A written by model B from A's outputs. If third-person transfers equally well, there's no privileged self-access — a clean negative result. Both outcomes are informative, which is the property you want in a weekend project. It also slots naturally into your battery framing as a self-model-fidelity battery, though I wouldn't oversell that link in the report.

Where it most likely dies, and the day-1 kill-test: ceiling effects. Post-trained assistants across labs already agree heavily on preference items, so B may start near 0.85+, leaving no room to detect anything. First three hours of the sprint: run the battery baseline across two models with generic prompts. If agreement is at ceiling, either rebuild the item set around measured disagreement (items selected because models actually diverge) or kill the project — do not proceed on the full grid first. Standard hygiene otherwise: order-flipped pairwise items, fixed temperature, multiple samples, paraphrase controls on the descriptions.

Feasibility is fine: pure API work, roughly 200 items × 5 samples × ~8 cells × 3 models ≈ low tens of thousands of short calls, no GPUs required; a 2–3 person team covers it in two days. For items, the CAIS utility-engineering battery style (which the sprint page itself links) gives you infrastructure to adapt rather than invent.
